# Sound Human

## The card

1. Name the one reader and what they can do or learn from this that they didn't know before. If you can't name them, ask.
2. Write the two persona lines (Before drafting), then draft as that person talking to one reader across a table.
3. Look in the brief for what puts a person in, and use what's there.
4. The first line, the ending and the ask follow the By format table.
5. Chain every sentence to the one before it.
6. Trace every detail to the brief and cut what you can't.
7. Hold the hard floor.
8. Read it aloud. That is the governing test.
9. Watch for the wrong fix: tidy, correct, every fact present and nobody home is still a failure.
10. Draft fast, in one go. The passes come after the draft.

## Before drafting

The writer is the person the brief is about, speaking as I, unless the brief says otherwise. Before drafting, write two lines about the writer as a person: what they'd never say, and what they'd say twice. Use the samples if you have them and the brief if you don't. Then draft as that person talking to one reader across a table. When the writer's voice changes by channel, use the samples for this channel. With samples, read 300 words or more first. With none, draft from the brief, keep every missing name and figure as a plain noun, and add one line at the end saying a few hundred words of the writer's own text would let you match their voice.

Look in the brief for what puts a person in: something the writer admits, a figure they'd swear to, a thing with a name, a point of view they'd argue. Use what's there. If the piece needs one and the brief lacks it, ask the writer up to three short questions in one message before drafting (what went wrong first, which number would you swear to, what did someone actually say). If they can't answer, write what the brief supports and keep it short. When the writer asked for a length the brief can't fill, write what it supports and list the questions that would fill the rest. Reports, DMs and short replies need none of it.

## Precedence and the hard floor

Order of authority: what the writer says in this conversation, then their voice file or samples, then this skill. The voice file decides texture: vocabulary, openers, sign-offs, sentence shape and slang. The hard floor holds over all of it:

- Em dashes, en dashes, double dashes and spaced hyphens: none. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses or a new sentence.
- Semicolons: none. A period or "and" replaces them, never a bare comma.
- The fillers genuinely, honestly and truly: none.
- Antithesis and mirrored reframes: zero in written copy. A spoken script may use one flip, as the hook or the close, when the speaker takes a side and the script backs up the second half.
- Engagement-bait questions: none.
- Invented facts: none, and that includes urgency or scarcity that isn't literally true and dated.

"Really" and "actually" are padding by default and allowed when the samples use them. Spelling follows the voice file, then the user, then US, and never drifts inside a piece.

## By format

| Format | First line | Ending | Ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Social post (LinkedIn, X, Instagram) | The reader, by who they are or their situation, and the result or promise, written to the fold. The second line gives the reason to tap "more": the cost or the stakes. | The scene, or the reader's next move as a fact from the brief. No moral. | Most posts none. One in five may add one line after the ending pointing to the newsletter or product. |
| Newsletter | The writer, as I, and one thing that happened to them. Turn to "you" by line three or four. | One concrete action with a when or a number, or an invitation to reply. | One, plainly, or in the P.S. |
| Essay or long-form | The first scene, or the turning moment and then back to the start. | The scene. | None, or a P.S. |
| Spoken script | The hook. It or the line after it says "you". | The close, one line: how to get the thing, a side to pick, whether they'd use it, or the one action from the value. The story finishes on a scene before the close. | One. The mention and a close that says how to get the thing count as one. |
| Email, one to one | Pays off the subject line. | The ask, then the sign-off. | One, in a full sentence, saying what they get and what it costs them. |
| Report, memo or update (including Slack and Teams) | The news, or the decision needed. | The decision or next step, with an owner and a date. | One, to one named person. |
| DM or short reply | The answer. | Where the answer ends. Shorter than the message it answers. | Only if needed. |

For a published post or a subject line, draft ten first lines and keep the most specific. For a script, draft five hooks of different types and keep the one the viewer can't scroll past. When the writer names a format they've used before, or pastes a past post that did well, keep its structure and change the substance. A numbered list of lessons from real work is a valid post when each item carries a figure or a moment from the brief.

## Story and flow

A report orders facts by importance, so its sentences can trade places. A story runs in the order it was lived and is built around one moment. Every piece carries one idea, and a scene that doesn't serve it goes.

The chain: because, so, until, which meant, by the time and and then carry the reader between facts, and no cut removes them. If a reader who wasn't there would ask "and then what?" between two sentences, the link is missing.

Give the liveliest scene a line of what was said or seen, and put a reward (a quote, a figure, a turn) about every 150 words. When a run of sentences takes the same breath, rewrite it, since a short sentence lands hardest after a long one. Vary paragraph length and let one stop short.

## Words, punctuation and lists

Tier 1 words never appear in your own prose, and Tier 2 words are fine alone. When a sentence breaks a rule, rebuild it, because a synonym with the same energy leaves the tell in place.

An ellipsis may join two linked ideas ("slow to start...quick once it runs") and never trails off. Use one exclamation mark per 1,000 words at most. The formatting tells are in the tells file.

A factual list has as many items as there are facts. A decorative triad ("cleaner, faster, sharper") or a paired adjective ("warm and welcoming") shrinks to the one word that does the work.

## Substance

- Name things when the brief names them. When it doesn't, keep the noun plain ("the florist"). A plain true sentence beats a vivid false one.
- Use exact amounts wherever they exist ("last Thursday" over "recently").
- A sourced story, such as a named case from a book, counts as a specific.
- When editing someone's text, keep every fact and add none.
- Let something stay broken when it is, and never invent a flaw for texture.
- The hedge fix: keep a qualifier that makes the sentence true ("for shops under ten staff") and cut one that only makes it safe ("it could be argued that").
- Make statements. The allowed questions are a real request in a full sentence ("Would Wednesday afternoon work for a call?"), a script's "would you use this", and a rare self-reflection question the content has earned.

## Texture

Use contractions. Start a sentence with And, But or So sometimes, and use fragments on purpose. Bring back the slang the writer uses, and never plant typos, forced slang or stray hedges to seem human. The voice file sets the swearing ceiling. Default: "damn".

## Self-check

Work silently, in order. Publication runs all twelve passes. A daily social post runs 1, 2, 3, 4 and 9. Chat replies, reports and DMs run 1, 3, 4, 5 and 9.

1. **Out loud.** Rewrite every sentence you wouldn't say to the one reader.
2. **So what.** The one reader, and what they can do or learn.
3. **Tells.** Every banned word, phrase and structural tell, fixed by restructuring.
4. **Punctuation.** Dashes, semicolons, trailing ellipses, curly quotes in social copy, stray markdown, chatbot citation junk, placeholder brackets.
5. **Flow.** No run on one breath, no wall of one-liners, and a shuffle would break it.
6. **Lists.** No decorative triads or paired adjectives.
7. **Specifics.** Exact amounts, one detail true only here.
8. **Commitment.** The hedge fix, and no reflexive counterpoints.
9. **Truth.** Open every link and check every named source exists. Trace every detail, elaborations included (a time of day, how or why something works, what someone saw, a pronoun, a claim wider than the brief's), and cut what you can't. A guess you flag in a note is still a guess, so cut it.
10. **Cut.** Copy over 200 words loses about a fifth. Reports and notes lose padding only.
11. **Format.** First line, ending, ask and formatting match the By format table and the channels file.
12. **Person.** A particular person is talking, with no preaching or performance. The writer could stand behind every sentence and answer a follow-up question on any of them.

The work is finished when the one reader can act on it, and every fact in it is one the writer would repeat to their face.
