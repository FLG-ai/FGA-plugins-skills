# Examples

These show what to check. Never reuse their shape: line-break pattern, opener type, length or closing move.

Every specific and every pronoun in a right version traces to the brief above it, which is the standard for your own drafts too. Each wrong version shows a failure a model makes on that brief.

## 1. The same brief with samples and without

Brief from the writer: Bea, bike repair shop. In June she ran the first Saturday bring-your-own-bike clinic, £10 a slot, where people fix their own puncture with her talking them through it. Four people booked and seven turned up. One man brought a bike with a flat that had been sat in his shed since 2019, and he fixed it himself. She ran out of inner tubes by 11am and has since ordered 40 more. She says the hardest part was keeping her hands off the tyre levers. Next clinic is Saturday 5 July, and slots are booked on the site. Instagram caption.

Her samples, about 200 words from earlier captions:

> So the shop's been open a year this week and I've just worked out I've fixed 1,140 punctures. Give or take. That's a lot of tubes.
>
> Things I've learned. Nobody knows what size their wheels are. Everyone thinks their brakes are fine and they're really not. And the bike that's been in the shed since lockdown is always the one someone wants back by Friday.
>
> I still love it though. Had a kid in yesterday, maybe nine, who'd tried to fix his own chain with a spoon. A spoon. We sorted it and he rode off looking so proper pleased with himself that I forgot to charge him.
>
> Then a bloke came in at ten to five with a noise he couldn't describe and I couldn't hear. I can't diagnose a noise over the phone either, I've tried.
>
> Anyway. Open Tuesday to Saturday, 9 till 5. Bring the bike. Cheers, Bea

With samples:

> If you've got a puncture you keep putting off, £10 and I'll talk you through fixing it yourself.
> 4 people booked the first clinic on Saturday, 7 turned up, and I ran out of tubes by 11.
>
> Hardest part for me was keeping my hands off the tyre levers.
>
> One bloke brought in a bike with a flat that's been sat in his shed since 2019. He fixed it himself.
>
> Anyway. Next one's Saturday 5 July, slots are on the site, and I've ordered 40 more tubes.
>
> #bikerepair #puncturerepair #fixyourownbike

Without samples:

> If you have a puncture you keep putting off, my £10 clinic lets you fix it yourself while I talk you through it.
> Four people booked the first one on Saturday, seven turned up, and I ran out of inner tubes by 11am.
>
> The hardest part for me was keeping my hands off the tyre levers.
>
> One man brought a bike with a flat that had been sat in his shed since 2019, and he fixed it himself.
>
> The next clinic is Saturday 5 July, and slots are on the site.
>
> #bikerepair #puncturerepair #fixyourownbike

A few hundred words of your own captions would let me match how you talk. This one is plain on purpose.

The samples bring "bloke", "Anyway.", dropped subjects and the short second thoughts. The no-samples version keeps the same facts, the same admission and the same ending, and adds no slang it can't point to.

## 2. The chain rule

Brief from the writer: Ines, pottery studio, took kiln bookings by text. In May two people turned up for the same Saturday firing slot, twice. Built an online booking form in one evening, expected regulars to ignore it. First week: 14 of 16 bookings came through the form. One regular wrote that she liked seeing which slots were free. Still gets the odd text.

Before, with every fact present and nothing linking them:

> Ines runs a pottery studio. She took kiln bookings by text. In May two people turned up for the same Saturday slot, twice. She built an online booking form in one evening. She worried the regulars would ignore it. In the first week 14 of 16 bookings came through the form. One regular said she liked seeing which slots were free. Ines still gets the odd text.

After, with the same facts joined and nothing added:

> Ines used to take kiln bookings by text, and in May that went wrong twice, both times with two people turning up for the same Saturday firing slot. So she spent one evening building a booking form, expecting her regulars to ignore it. In the first week 14 of her 16 bookings came through the form, and one regular wrote to say she liked seeing which slots were free. Ines still gets the odd text.

"So", "and" and "in the first week" do the joining, and the regular's message gets the most room because it's the moment with life in it.

## 3. A LinkedIn post, with ten first lines and two right shapes

Brief from the writer: Priya, bookkeeper. One café client sent every receipt as a WhatsApp photo, about 40 a month. Priya admits she put the matching off until the last Saturday of the month, then scrolled back through the thread matching each photo to a bank line, which took the whole day. In March she spent 20 minutes setting the owner up with a shared folder and a scan-to-folder app on her phone. The owner's first question was whether the app cost anything. It doesn't. April: three receipts by WhatsApp, the rest in the folder. Month-end for that client now takes two hours. The owner said she takes the photo while the printer is still warm. Reader: other bookkeepers at small firms. No ask.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> Month-end for one of my café clients used to take a full Saturday. The owner sent every receipt as a WhatsApp photo, around 40 a month, and I matched each one to a bank line by scrolling back through the thread.
>
> In March I set up a shared folder and a scan-to-folder app on her phone. It took 20 minutes. In April, three receipts came by WhatsApp and the rest were in the folder. Month-end now takes two hours.
>
> She says she takes the photo while the printer is still warm.
>
> Small changes to how receipts arrive can save bookkeepers hours every month.

Every fact is there, the last line explains the scene, nobody admits anything and no one is talking.

Ten first lines, drafted before the post:

1. I lost a Saturday a month to receipt photos until March.
2. I ran month-end for one café from a WhatsApp thread of 40 receipt photos.
3. Bookkeepers, my month-end for one café went from a full Saturday to two hours.
4. A café client sent me 40 receipts a month as WhatsApp photos.
5. One café client cost me a Saturday every month until March.
6. One 20-minute setup gave me back a Saturday a month.
7. My café client takes the photo now while the printer is still warm.
8. If you do month-end from a WhatsApp thread, this took me 20 minutes to fix.
9. Forty receipt photos, one thread, one whole Saturday, every month.
10. Bookkeepers, three receipt photos came by WhatsApp in April instead of 40.

The eighth names the reader by their situation and promises a fix with a figure, so it opens the first version. The third names them by job title with the result, and opens the second.

Right, ending on the scene:

> If you do month-end from a WhatsApp thread, this took me 20 minutes to fix.
> One café sent me about 40 receipt photos a month, and matching them cost me a whole Saturday.
>
> I'd put it off until the last Saturday of the month, then scroll back through the thread pairing each photo with a bank line.
>
> In March I spent 20 minutes setting the owner up with a shared folder and a scan-to-folder app on her phone.
>
> Her first question was whether the app cost anything. It doesn't.
>
> In April three receipts came by WhatsApp and the rest were in the folder. Month-end for her now takes me two hours.
>
> She told me she takes the photo while the printer is still warm.

Right, shorter, ending on the reader's move:

> Bookkeepers, my month-end for one café went from a full Saturday to two hours.
> The owner sent every receipt as a WhatsApp photo, about 40 a month, and I kept putting the matching off.
>
> In March I set her up with a shared folder and a scan-to-folder app on her phone. It took 20 minutes, and the app is free.
>
> In April three receipts came by WhatsApp.
>
> If a client still sends you receipt photos, that setup takes 20 minutes and the app costs nothing.

In both, the first two lines fit above the fold and the second one carries the cost. The first ends on what the owner said. The second ends on the reader's next move, built from facts in the brief.

## 4. A spoken script, 45 seconds

Brief from the writer: Tomas, dog-walking service. In 2023 he walked every dog himself and turned down eleven dog owners in one month because he wouldn't hire. He says he was scared to trust anyone else with a client's dog. A neighbor asked three times, and on the third ask he said yes and wrote a job ad that evening. His first walker did two shadow walks with him before taking a dog alone, paid for both, and on the second walk a dog lunged at them on a narrow path. He lost one client that first month, a man who wanted Tomas and only Tomas. Six walkers work with him now. He sells a checklist for starting a dog-walking business, and the shadow plan is page two of it. Close: comment WALK for the link.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> In 2023 I turned down eleven dog owners in one month because I wouldn't hire anyone. A neighbor asked three times before I said yes. My first walker did two paid shadow walks with me before taking a dog alone. I lost one client who only wanted me. Today six walkers work with me. My checklist for starting a dog-walking business includes the shadow plan on page two. Comment WALK for the link.

Every fact, no "you", nothing for the viewer to do, and the sentences could be read in any order.

Five hooks, each a different type:

- Call-out: "If you're turning down dog owners because you won't hire, I turned down eleven in one month."
- Confession: "I was scared to let anyone else walk a client's dog, so I turned away eleven owners in a month."
- Result: "I went from walking every dog myself to six walkers."
- A moment: "On a new walker's second shadow walk, a dog lunged at us on a narrow path."
- Bold opinion: "Pay your new walker for the walks where they only watch."

The bold opinion is the pick. It takes a side, and the rest of the script backs it up.

Right, one breath per line:

> Pay your new walker for the walks where they only watch.
>
> If you're turning down dog owners because you won't hire, I turned down ELEVEN in one month.
>
> In 2023 I walked every dog myself, because I was scared to trust anyone else with a client's dog.
>
> A neighbor asked me three times, and on the third ask I said yes and wrote a job ad that evening.
>
> So before your first walker takes a dog alone, do two walks together, and PAY them for both.
>
> On our second one a dog lunged at us on a narrow path.
>
> That plan's page two of the checklist I sell for starting a dog-walking business, anyway.
>
> I lost one client that first month, a man who wanted me and only me.
>
> Six walkers work with me now.
>
> Comment WALK and I'll send you the link.

When the brief names a feeling, the script says it once, in the speaker's own words. The mention sits inside the teaching, the story finishes on a scene, and the close only says how to get the thing.

## 5. A newsletter opening

Brief from the writer: Jo, weekly newsletter for people who run small shops. On Tuesday her card terminal stopped taking contactless halfway through a queue, and she spent 40 minutes on hold with the provider before the fix turned out to be switching it off and on twice. So this week she rang four of her suppliers to see who answers the phone, and only one picked up inside a minute. That test is the issue. She wants readers to ring their own suppliers before they need them. She signs off "Jo".

Wrong, straight from a model:

> Subject: What a week!
>
> Preview: Grab a coffee, this one's a long one
>
> Happy Friday! What a week it's been. Tuesday was one of those days where everything goes wrong at once, and I nearly lost it. Anyway, it got me thinking about suppliers...

It's all diary, it invents a Friday and a near-meltdown, and the preview teases.

Right, the subject, the preview and the first four lines:

> Subject: 40 minutes on hold to switch it off and on
>
> Preview: I rang four suppliers. One answered inside a minute.
>
> On Tuesday my card terminal stopped taking contactless halfway through a queue.
>
> I spent 40 minutes on hold with the provider, and the fix was switching it off and on twice.
>
> So this week I rang four of my suppliers to see who answers, and only one picked up inside a minute.
>
> Ring your own before you need them, and here's how my four did.

It opens on Jo and one thing that happened, turns to the reader on line four, and the preview says what the reader gets.

## 6. A sourced story in a client note

Brief from the writer: Sam, runs a small accounting practice, writes a short weekly note to clients. Last week a client asked him whether £50 a month into a pension was worth the paperwork. He wants to answer with Ronald Read, from the introduction to Morgan Housel's The Psychology of Money (2020): Read worked at a gas station and later as a janitor in a department store in Vermont, bought shares in companies he understood and left them alone for decades, died in 2014 aged 92, and left about $8 million, most of it to his local hospital and library. After Read, Sam wants one line telling clients to keep the £50 going.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> A client asked me last week whether £50 a month into a pension was worth the paperwork. It reminded me of Ronald Read, whose story opens Morgan Housel's The Psychology of Money. Read worked at a gas station and as a janitor, invested in companies he understood, and held on for decades. When he died in 2014 at 92, he left about $8 million, most of it to his local hospital and library. Read's story shows what small, regular amounts can do over a long time. So yes, your £50 a month matters more than you might think.

The story is right, and the last two lines explain it back to the reader with a moral and a vague reassurance.

Right:

> Last week a client asked me whether £50 a month into a pension was worth the paperwork.
>
> I told them about Ronald Read. Housel opens The Psychology of Money with him. Read worked at a gas station in Vermont and later as a janitor in a department store, and he bought shares in companies he understood and left them alone for decades. He died in 2014, aged 92, and left about $8 million, most of it to his local hospital and library.
>
> If you're putting £50 a month in, keep it going and leave it alone.

Read's story carries the argument, and the last line answers the question with the figure in it. The brief gives no gender for the client, so the note says "them".

## 7. A report paragraph

Brief from the writer: note to the office manager. The floor 2 printer jammed 14 times in August according to the service log, 11 of them on double-sided jobs. The technician replaced the duplex roller on 3 September, the visit cost $140, and there have been no jams in the 12 working days since. Recommendation: the office manager keeps double-sided printing switched on and records any new jam in the same log. The writer will check the log again on 1 October.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> This note summarizes the printer situation on floor 2. During August, a total of 14 jams were recorded, the majority of them on double-sided jobs. Following a technician visit on 3 September, during which the duplex roller was replaced at a cost of $140, no further jams have been observed over the subsequent 12 working days. It is therefore recommended that double-sided printing remain enabled and that any future jams be logged consistently to support future maintenance decisions.

Every fact is in, the note announces itself, the verbs hide in nouns, and "the majority" replaces a figure the log gave.

Right, and neutral because the format is a report:

> The floor 2 printer jammed 14 times in August according to the service log, and 11 of those jams were on double-sided jobs. The technician replaced the duplex roller on 3 September, at $140 for the visit, and the printer has run 12 working days since with no jam logged. Please keep double-sided printing switched on and record any new jam in the same log, and I'll check it again on 1 October.

The news is in the first two lines, and it ends on the next step with an owner and a date. A first-person aside about dreading the printer would be the wrong fix here.

## 8. A team update on Slack

Brief from the writer: Ade, head of customer support, posting in the support team channel. The new returns form went live in the pilot store on 2 September. In three weeks, return emails needing a reply fell from 140 to 60. It rolls out to all six stores on Monday 12 October. He needs Lena in legal to sign off the refund wording by Wednesday 7 October.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> Hi team! 👋 Quick update on the returns form project. As many of you know, we've been piloting a new returns form in one of our stores over the past few weeks, and the results have been really encouraging. Return emails requiring a response have dropped significantly. We're hoping to roll this out more widely soon, pending a few approvals. Let me know if you have any questions!

The news waits until line three, "significantly" stands in for 140 and 60, and nobody owns the approval or has a date.

Right:

> The new returns form goes to all six stores on Monday 12 October.
> In the pilot store it cut return emails needing a reply from 140 to 60 in three weeks.
> @Lena, can you sign off the refund wording by Wednesday 7 October?

The news is the first line, the figure proves it, and the post ends on one ask to one named person with a date.

## 9. A DM reply

Brief from the writer: a follower asked by DM whether her Notion template runs on Notion's free plan. It does, and she built it on a free account. Her DMs are short and start with "yep" when the answer is yes.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> Hi, thanks for asking. Yes, the template works on Notion's free plan. I built it on a free account myself, so you won't need to upgrade to use it. Let me know if you have any other questions.

Right:

> Yep, it runs on the free plan. I built it on one.

The fix is shorter than the draft and answers in the writer's own opener. A DM that gets longer in the fix has gone wrong.

## 10. A cold email

Brief from the writer: Leo, who signs emails "Leo", writing cold to Nadia, who posted about losing Sunday evenings to class scheduling. Leo's team helped a four-room yoga studio cut that from five hours a week to about 45 minutes, and the setup took one afternoon. Ask for 20 minutes on Tuesday or Wednesday.

Wrong, straight from a model:

> Subject: Class scheduling
>
> Hi Nadia,
>
> I noticed your recent post about class scheduling taking up your Sunday evenings. We work with studios to reduce the time spent on scheduling. One four-room yoga studio we worked with reduced their weekly scheduling time from five hours to about 45 minutes, and setup took one afternoon. I'd be happy to share more if useful. Would you be open to a 20-minute call on Tuesday or Wednesday?
>
> Best regards,
> Leo

Right:

> Subject: Five hours of scheduling down to 45 minutes
>
> Hi Nadia,
>
> I saw your post about losing Sunday evenings to class scheduling. We helped a four-room yoga studio cut that from five hours a week to about 45 minutes, and the setup took one afternoon. If you can spare 20 minutes on Tuesday or Wednesday, I'll walk you through what they changed.
>
> Leo

The subject line says what happened, the first line pays it off, and the sign-off is how Leo signs a text.
